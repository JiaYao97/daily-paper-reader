# Kernel-based learning of manifold-to-manifold maps from scattered data

Abstract 

We describe and analyze new methods for approximating manifold-to-manifold maps using only scattered data information. To this end, we first study kernel-based approximation meth-ods for scalar-valued functions defined on a manifold and derive a new sampling inequality and error estimates for functions from Sobolev spaces. After that, these methods are combined with a closest point projection to reconstruct manifold-to-manifold maps. The new methods are analyzed and error estimates are derived. Finally, numerical examples are given to verify the theoretical findings. 

Keywords: kernel-based learning, scattered data, manifold-valued functions 

# 1 Introduction 

Interpolation and approximation with positive definite kernels provides a powerful framework for reconstructing a multivariate function f : Ω → R from known function values at a finite set of data sites scattered throughout the domain of definition Ω ⊆ Rd. Since these methods were first proposed, they have been generalized in various directions. On the one hand, numerous techniques have been developed for defining positive definite kernels on more specific domains of definition, such as smooth manifolds M, thereby enabling the reconstruction of functions f : M → R (see, for example, [15, 22, 20, 6]). On the other hand, the theory of positive definite kernels and the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces has been extended from the scalar-valued setting to vector- and, more generally, Hilbert space-valued functions, thus allowing the approximation of mappings f : Ω → W , where W is a Hilbert space [30, 21, 5, 11]. Motivated by applications in numerous scientific and engineering domains, recent years have seen a growing interest in approximation methods for functions f : Ω → N , whose codomain N possesses the structure of a smooth manifold rather than that of a vector space. Such mappings arise, for

Projection-based methods [9, 13, 17], where first an interpolant is constructed in the ambient space of an embedded submanifold, and the resulting values are subsequently projected onto the manifold by means of a suitable mapping; 2. Tangent space methods (Push-Interpolate-Pull methods) [36, 37, 39], where the given data are first mapped to a linear space - typically the tangent space at a chosen base point - followed by classical vector-valued interpolation and finally mapping back the reconstructed values to the manifold via an inverse transformation; 3. Riemannian mean-based methods [12, 14], where the evaluation of the interpolant involves computing a weighted mean of function values in N through nonlinear optimization. There also exist several techniques that do not fall directly into any of the aforementioned categories (e.g., [16]), or that combine multiple of the above approaches [32]. With the exception of [14], however, these methods require the sample locations to have a certain structure. In most cases, it is assumed that the function values are known on some type of regular grid. Without such a grid structure, these methods cannot be applied. Fortunately, in this situation the ideas from kernel-based interpolation of scalar-valued functions on manifolds, vector-valued kernel-based interpolation and manifold-valued approaches can be naturally combined into a method for reconstructing functions between manifolds. In this work, we derive such methods, i.e., we address the problem of reconstructing a manifold-valued function f : M → N , where M and 

N are compact smooth manifolds, from known values f (x1), . . . , f (xN ) ∈ N at a set of scattered data sites X = {x1, . . . , x N } ⊆ M . To our knowledge, this problem has not been investigated in this form until now. In principle, various kernel-based approximation techniques could be combined with any of the aforementioned methods for manifold-valued interpolation. However, we restrict our attention to kernel-based interpolation (see [8]) and penalized least-squares approximation on embedded submanifolds M. In both cases, the kernel on M is obtained by restricting a positive definite kernel defined on the ambient space to the submanifold. Moreover, we employ a projection approach to ensure that the resulting interpolant indeed takes values in the target manifold N .The remainder of this article is organized as follows: In the next section, we discuss kernel-based approximation methods for scalar-valued functions, defined on some domain Ω ⊆ Rd. In the third section, we extend these results to kernel-based approximations for scalar-valued functions defined on embedded submanifolds. To this end, we first collect some necessary material on embedded submanifolds in Subsection 3.1 and then state and prove results on the norm-minimal interpolation and penalized least-squares processes. While we can rely on previous work for the interpolation operator, the results on the penalized least-squares approximation are new in this context. Par-ticularly, we discuss their approximation properties if the measured data contain noise. Section 4 then contains the core of this paper, where the kernel-based approximation methods are combined 2with a closest point projection to define manifold-to-manifold approximations. Finally, in Section 5, we numerically illustrate these results with two academic examples and a real-world application from crystallography. 

# 2 Kernel-based approximation 

Kernel-based learning or approximation is a well-established tool in approximation theory and statistics, it comes in the form of scattered data approximation with radial basis functions (see for example [4, 7, 34]), Gaussian processes (see for example [27]) and support vector machines (see for example [29, 31]). Usually, in this context, functions f : Ω → R that are defined on some domain Ω ⊆ Rd

are learned or approximated using only information f (x1), . . . , f (xN ) measured at scattered data locations X = {x1, . . . , x N } ⊆ Ω. In this section, we will shortly review the material relevant to us. Here, we follow mainly [34]. Kernel-based learning of functions f : Ω → R usually assumes that the unknown function comes from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space H. This is a Hilbert space of real-valued functions defined on Ω, possessing a kernel K : Ω ×Ω → R satisfying K(·, x ) ∈ H for all x ∈ Ωand f (x) = ⟨f, K (·, x )⟩H for all x ∈ Ω and all f ∈ H. ## 3.2 Kernel-based approximation of functions defined on manifolds 

Compared to the situation in Section 2, we are now interested in domains Ω = M, where M is a smooth, compact and connected m-dimensional embedded submanifold of Rd. Of course, the operators IX and QX,λ from Definition 2.1 are well-defined if Ω = M and now become operators from C(M) to VX and Lemma 2.2 still holds. However, we now have to specify what kind of positive definite kernels on M we want to use. Although there are different ways to construct kernels, particularly for specific manifolds (see for example [15, 22, 20, 6]), we will focus on kernels which arise by restricting positive definite kernels defined on the ambient space Rd to the embedded submanifold M, following particularly [8]. Consider a positive definite kernel K : Rd ×Rd → R of the form K(x, y ) = Φ( x−y) = ϕ(∥x−y∥2). Restricting it to the compact submanifold M ⊆ Rd, gives the kernel Ψ( ·, ·) := K(·, ·)|M×M : M × M → R,

which is obviously positive definite on M. For our error analysis we need to know the corresponding reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Fortunately, it turns out that if we start with a kernel K of this form which is the reproducing kernel of a Sobolev space then the restricted kernel Ψ will also be the reproducing kernel of a Sobolev space on the manifold M. ## 4.1 Closest point approximation 

In this subsection, we will recall and prove relevant material on the closest point projection PN :

Rℓ → N . In particular, we are interested in its well-definedness. Let u ∈ Rℓ. A point p∗ ∈ N is called a closest point to u from N if p∗ = arg min p∈N ∥u − p∥2.

Since N is assumed to be compact, a closest point to u ∈ Rℓ always exist. However, it does not have to be unique. As in the situation of best approximations from linear subspaces, closest points are characterized by some type of orthogonality. 

Lemma 4.1. Let p ∈ N be a closest point to u ∈ Rℓ. Then u − p ∈ NpN .

Proof. Let v ∈ TpN . Consider an arbitrary curve c : ( −ϵ, ϵ ) → N with c(0) = p and ˙ c(0) = v. 

As p is a closest point to u, the function g : ( −ϵ, ϵ ) → R, t 7 → ∥ u − c(t)∥22 has a local minimum at 

t = 0. Hence, 0 = ˙ g(0) = ddt |t=0 ⟨u − c(t), u − c(t)⟩2 = 2 ⟨c(0) , ˙c(0) ⟩2 − 2⟨u, ˙c(0) ⟩2 = 2 ⟨u − p, v ⟩2 and therefore u − p ⊥ v. □

Taking a closer look at the unit sphere Sℓ−1 ⊆ Rℓ, we note that for each u ∈ Rℓ \{ 0} there exists a unique closest point from Sℓ−1, which is given by u 7 → u/ ∥u∥2. Hence, in this case there exists an open neighborhood U of Sℓ−1, such that each u ∈ U has a unique closest point from Sℓ−1. This is no particularity of the sphere, but holds in general. Such a U is called a tubular neighborhood, see [19, Chapter 6]. 

Definition 4.2. A tubular neighborhood of an embedded submanifold N ⊆ Rℓ is a neighborhood 

U ⊆ Rℓ of N which is the diffeomorphic image under the addition map E : N N → Rℓ, (p, n ) 7 → p+n,of an open subset V ⊆ N N of the form V = {(p, n ) ∈ N N : ∥n∥2 < δ (p)} for some positive continuous function δ : N → R. If the function δ can be chosen as constant, δ ≡ ˆδ > 0, we say that 

U = E(V ) is a uniform tubular neighborhood of N .

The tubular neighborhood theorem, [19, Theorem 6.24]), guarantees that every embedded sub-manifold of Rℓ has a tubular neighborhood. 

Theorem 4.3 (Tubular Neighborhood Theorem) . Every embedded submanifold N of Rℓ has a tubular neighborhood. If N is compact, there also exists a uniform tubular neighborhood of N .

## 4.2 Kernel-based learning combined with closest point projections 

We now come back to studying the interpolation and approximation operators IX, N = PN ◦ IX, Rℓ

and QX,λ, N = PN ◦ QX,λ, Rℓ . They are using a restricted kernel Ψ : M × M → R, Ψ( x, y ) = Φ( x − y) for each component. This kernel is supposed to be a reproducing kernel of Hσ (M)with σ > max {m/ 2, 1}, which is satisfied if Φ has a Fourier transform satisfying (2.1) with τ > 

max {d/ 2, (d − m)/2 + 1 } as we have σ = τ − (d − m)/2. The condition on τ is for example satisfied if τ > d/ 2 and m ≥ 2 or if τ > (d + 1) /2. To ensure our operators to be well-defined, we need that IX, Rℓ f (x) and QX,λ, Rℓ f (x) belong to a uniform tubular neighborhood U of M. We will use standard L∞-error estimates to achieve this. To apply these estimates, we need the target function f : M → N to belong to a Sobolev space 

Hβ (M, Rℓ). Thus, we aim to derive error bounds for interpolating functions from the set 

Hβ (M, N ) := {f ∈ Hβ (M, Rℓ) : f (x) ∈ N almost everywhere }.

Unfortunately, using standard L∞-error estimates to ensure that IX, Rℓ f and QX,λ, Rℓ f map M

into the tubular neighborhood U of N also means that the necessary fill distance will depend on the function f . One way of avoiding this is to restrict ourselves to functions f ∈ Hβ (M, N ) satisfying 

∥f ∥Hβ (M,Rℓ) ≤ 1, where 1 can be replaced by any other constant, which, of course, only shifts the problem to identifying whether f has a bounded norm or not. ℓ∞(X)-norm. Let us conclude our theoretical discussion with a few remarks concerning these error estimates. In general, it is not possible to quantify the radius ˆδ of the uniform tubular neighborhood U of N .However, as we shall see in the next section, such estimates for ˆδ can sometimes be obtained once a specific manifold N is fixed. Moreover, even when ˆδ is known, we cannot determine how small the constant h0 must be chosen, since the norm ∥f ∥Hσ (M,Rℓ) of the unknown function f is typically not available. Depending on the particular choice of the compact embedded submanifold N , one must also address how the projection operator PN can be implemented numerically. For certain manifolds, 20 efficient methods to compute best approximations for points within tubular neighborhoods exist. An example will be presented in Section 5. The choices q = ∞ and μ = 0 in our error estimates, give bounds for the pointwise error. Because of Definition 3.13 this error is measured in the Euclidean norm of the ambient space Rℓ

of N . However, for a connected, compact embedded submanifold N ⊆ Rℓ this distance is strongly equivalent to the intrinsic distance dN : N × N → R according to [8, Theorem 6], so that we can expect the same convergence order in this metric. More generally, for compact manifolds arbitrary Riemannian metrics induce strongly equivalent distances and our error bounds hold analogously. So far we have only considered embedded submanifolds of Euclidean spaces. However, our method can easily be generalized to arbitrary compact manifolds M and N . The Nash embedding theorem [24] guarantees that every m-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold can be isometri-cally embedded into some Rd with d ≤ m(3 m + 11) /2. The image ι(M) ⊆ Rd of such an embedding is an m-dimensional submanifold of Rd, which is compact if M is compact. Choosing isometric em-beddings ι1 : M → Rd and ι2 : N → Rℓ we can again restrict positive definite kernels on the ambient space Rd to positive definite kernels on ι1(M). As ι1 is an isometry, we get hX, M = hι1(X),ι 1(M).At the same time, we can choose a uniform tubular neighborhood U of ι2(N ) ⊆ Rℓ, as well as an associated closest point projection. One obtains results analogous to those in Theorems 4.7 and 4.8. However, note that the choice of an embedding for a given compact manifold is far from trivial in general and obviously highly depends on the specific manifold. Even if such an embedding is known explicitly, it is not necessarily useful in practice: It is important that the corresponding tubular neighborhood is as big as possible, while at the same time the associated closest point projection is realizable numerically. Fortunately, for some manifolds appropriate embeddings and corresponding numerical realizations of the closest point projection are known. We quickly mention such an ex-ample in the following section. Of course, one also has to keep in mind the curse of dimensionality, when embedding the manifold M via the embedding ι1.
