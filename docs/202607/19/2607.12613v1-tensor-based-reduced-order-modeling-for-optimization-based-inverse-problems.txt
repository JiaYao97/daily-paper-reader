TENSOR-BASED REDUCED-ORDER MODELING FOR OPTIMIZATION-BASED INVERSE PROBLEMS ∗

SAHIDUL ISLAM † , ANDREAS MANG † , AND MAXIM OLSHANSKII †

Abstract. We develop a tensor reduced-order modeling (TROM) framework for optimization-based inverse problems governed by parameter-dependent dynamical systems. The approach ap-proximates the parameter-to-observation map directly in tensor-train format, using either TT-SVD or TT-Cross compression, and integrates the resulting representation into a regularized nonlinear least-squares formulation. Beyond accelerating forward evaluations, the low-rank tensor structure is used to reformulate the inverse problem in reduced coordinates, assemble the Gauss–Newton quantities without forming the full observation-space Jacobian, and perform TROM-based objective minimization over the discrete parameter grid. This tensor optimization step can be used either as a stand-alone approximate minimization procedure or as a data-informed initialization for a subsequent Gauss–Newton solve. The method is studied for two inverse problems: an inverse heat-transfer prob-lem in a heterogeneous medium, where the unknown parameters describe the locations of multiple low-conductivity inclusions, and a FitzHugh–Nagumo parameter-estimation problem with a highly nonconvex optimization landscape. Numerical experiments assess the effects of ROM approximation error, measurement noise, regularization, initialization, spatial discretization, and increasing param-eter dimension. The results show that TROM can reproduce the behavior of full-order inversion at a substantially reduced online cost. The experiments also demonstrate that reduced-coordinate inversion, tensor-based optimization, and appropriate regularization improve robustness in higher-dimensional, noisy, and strongly nonconvex regimes. 

Key words. inverse problems, reduced-order modeling, tensor-train decomposition, low-rank tensor approximation, Gauss–Newton method, regularized nonlinear least squares, parameter esti-mation

1. Introduction. Inverse problems for parameter-dependent forward models, including systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs) and ordinary dif-ferential equations (ODEs), arise in many applications where internal material prop-erties, geometric features, source terms, or model parameters must be inferred from indirect and noisy measurements [26, 52, 23, 35]. In computational practice, solv-ing such problems usually requires repeated evaluations of a high-fidelity forward model, often together with derivative or sensitivity information. This cost can be-come prohibitive when the forward model is large-scale, the parameter dimension is moderate or high, or the inverse problem must be solved repeatedly for different data realizations. These challenges have motivated extensive research on surrogate and reduced-order models (ROMs) for inverse problems, uncertainty quantification, and data assimilation; see, e.g., [11, 13, 5, 1, 48, 14]. Such approaches replace the ex-pensive parameter-to-observation map by a computationally efficient approximation while retaining sufficient accuracy for the inverse solve. The need for fast and reliable inverse solvers is particularly pronounced in digital-

Surrogate models are of particular importance if inference needs to happen in (close to) real-time. In this context, inverse problems are not isolated offline computations: they are a recurring component of the model-update loop. Hence, the online stage must be inexpensive, stable with respect to noise and model error, and capable of handling repeated optimization tasks under limited computational budgets. Classical ROMs typically exploit low-dimensional structure in the state, solution manifold, or input–output map, for example through projection-based model reduc-tion or reduced basis methods [1, 48, 21]. These techniques have been successfully used in large-scale inverse problems [13, 31, 5]. However, inverse problems with several unknown parameters introduce an additional difficulty: the parameter-to-observation map may need to be approximated over a multidimensional parameter domain. Direct sampling over a tensor-product grid quickly becomes infeasible, while local surrogates may not provide the robustness needed for globalized or multi-start optimization. Tensorial ROMs (TROMs) provide a natural way to address this difficulty by exploiting low-rank structure in the joint dependence of the observable on the model parameters. In particular, low-rank tensor representations, such as tensor-train (TT) decomposition [45], offer compact approximations of high-dimensional arrays and sup-port efficient tensor arithmetic, interpolation, and optimization. Low-rank tensor methods are now well developed in numerical analysis and high-dimensional approxi-mation [28, 19, 16], and tensor-train cross (TT-Cross) techniques can construct com-pressed representations by sampling only a subset of tensor entries [44, 50, 7]. TROMs have been developed for parametric dynamical systems [33, 34, 24]. Nevertheless, the systematic use of TROMs in inverse modeling remains comparatively unexplored, especially beyond the basic goal of accelerating forward evaluations. In this paper, we investigate TROMs for deterministic numerical inversion. Our focus is not only on replacing the full-order model (FOM) with a fast surrogate for forward solves, but also on exploiting the tensor structure in inverse-problem-specific components: Gauss–Newton (GN) sensitivity assembly, reduced-coordinate inversion, and data-informed initialization (see C1–C4 below). These uses are important because the performance of nonlinear inverse solvers depends not only on the cost of residual evaluation, but also on the quality of sensitivity information, the choice of regulariza-tion, and the basin of attraction of the initial iterate. We study the proposed approach on two deterministic inverse problems. The first is a parabolic heat-transfer problem in a heterogeneous medium with low-conductivity inclusions. The unknown parameter vector encodes the locations and radii of one, two, or three inclusions, leading to parameter dimensions D = 3 , 6, 9. The available data consist of time-dependent temperature observations at several sensors placed on the domain boundary. The second example is a FitzHugh–Nagumo parameter-estimation problem, where the unknown parameters enter a nonlinear excitable-dynamics model. Although this forward model is low-dimensional, the associated least-squares objective has a strongly nonconvex landscape and is sensitive to initialization [55]. Rather than constructing a reduced model for the full state, we approximate directly the parameter-to-observation map. The corresponding observation tensor is compressed in TT format using either TT-SVD [45] or interpolation techniques based on TT-Cross and AMEn-type ideas [44, 7]. The resulting surrogate is then used inside a regularized nonlinear least-squares formulation of the inverse problem.

In the inversion procedure, the full-order map f is replaced by a tensor reduced-order approximation bf , which introduces a TROM error εrom , bf (α) = f (α) + εrom (α). Thus, the effective data misfit contains both measurement and ROM contributions. This distinction is used later in the choice of discrepancy targets and regularization parameters. 

Other Related Work. Low-rank tensor techniques have also been used in deter-ministic and statistical inverse problems. For the latter, TT approximations have been used to represent high-dimensional probability densities and accelerate poste-rior sampling [6]. Related low-rank tensor reconstructions combined with transport maps have been developed for concentrated posterior densities arising in Bayesian inversion [8]. This line of work has been extended to deep, composed TT trans-port maps for high-dimensional and conditional Bayesian inference [3, 4]. Tensor methods have also been incorporated into ROM construction for parametric PDEs and dynamical systems, including interpolatory, projection-based, and non-intrusive TROMs [33, 34, 37, 54, 24] and TT reduced-basis methods [39]. Another active direction is nonlinear manifold reduction, where encoder–decoder architectures can be advantageous for handling solution manifolds with slowly decaying Kolmogorov width [17]; examples include convolutional-autoencoder ROMs [29], deep-learning ROMs for parametrized PDEs [12], and neural-network ROMs for inverse problems and parameter identification [25]. Closely related to our use of the surrogate for Ja-cobian information, derivative-informed neural network surrogates of parameter-to-observation maps have been developed for outer-loop applications such as Bayesian inversion [43]. Finally, several works show that inverse solvers can benefit from ROM constructions designed specifically for inference, not only from faster forward evalu-ations: examples include posterior-adaptive reduced bases for Bayesian inversion [5] and goal-oriented inference methods that build reduced spaces around the prediction or identification task [30].

2.1.1. TT-SVD for Order Reduction. In this approach, the TT tensor bT is constructed by computing truncated SVDs of a sequence of matricizations of T , i.e., matrices of size ( N0 · · · Nk) × (Nk+1 · · · ND ) obtained by unfolding T ; see [45]. If the truncation criterion in each SVD is defined by the threshold εTT /√D, εTT > 0, on the relative magnitude of the singular values, then the resulting TT tensor satisfies (2.4) ∥T − bT ∥ F ≤ εTT ∥T ∥ F .

Note that TT-SVD generally requires access to all entries of T . For high parameter dimensions, computing the full tensor T may be prohibitively expensive.

T by adaptively sampling only a small subset of its entries, thereby avoiding full tensor assembly. It combines ideas from TT-Cross interpolation [44] with alternating minimal energy (AMEn) enrichment [7], iteratively updating TT cores while select-ing informative multi-index sets via max-volume principles [15, 50]. 3.4. TROM optimization and initialization. The TROM representation can be used either to minimize the objective functional directly over the discrete param-eter domain or to construct a data-informed initial guess for a subsequent iterative inversion method. The preferable use is problem-dependent: the tensor-based mini-mizer may already provide an accurate reconstruction, or it may serve only to initialize a local method such as GN. 6. Conclusions. We demonstrated that TROM surrogates provide reliable and fast approximations of the parameter-to-observation map, with an approximation er-ror that can be estimated and incorporated into the inversion procedure. In the Tikhonov framework considered here, both TT-SVD and TT-Cross surrogates repro-duce the behavior of full-order inversion at a substantially lower online cost. The experiments also show that the TROM error should be treated as part of the effective data perturbation when selecting the regularization parameter, which leads to stable reconstructions for both noise-free and noisy observations. A central conclusion is that the low-rank multilinear structure is useful beyond fast forward evaluation. After orthogonalization of the first TT core, the TROM least-squares term can be minimized equivalently in reduced coordinates; the Gauss– Newton residuals and Jacobian products can be assembled from TT contractions without forming the full observation-space Jacobian; and the tensor representation can be used to solve discrete objective minimization problems in TT format. De-pending on the inverse problem, this tensor optimization step can serve either as a stand-alone approximate minimization procedure or as a data-informed initialization for a subsequent Gauss–Newton solve. The numerical results illustrate these roles in complementary settings. In the heat-transfer problem, the method remains ef-fective as the parameter dimension increases, and TT-Cross extends the approach to regimes where full tensor construction by TT-SVD is no longer practical. In the FitzHugh–Nagumo example, the objective landscape is strongly nonconvex and the Gauss–Newton method is sensitive to initialization; in this case, the TROM optimiza-tion step substantially improves robustness by identifying a better basin of attrac-tion. Overall, the experiments show that reduced-coordinate inversion, tensor-based objective minimization, and appropriate regularization improve robustness in higher-dimensional, noisy, and nonconvex inverse problems. The main tradeoff is the offline cost of constructing the TROM surrogate, espe-cially in higher parameter dimension. Once this surrogate is available, however, the online inverse solves are inexpensive.
